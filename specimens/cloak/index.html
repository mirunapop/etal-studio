How do tumours hide from the immune system?

Many tumours switch off the immune cells sent to destroy them. Five hospitals and twelve labs are filming how it happens, and testing whether patients' own immune cells can be kept working.

The question

The immune system can find a tumour. Then it stops.

T cells are the immune system's specialists in killing diseased cells, and they often find their way into a tumour. But once there, many of them go quiet: signals from the tumour wear them down until they stop attacking.

Immunotherapy can switch some of them back on. It helps some patients for years and others not at all, and it is still hard to predict who will respond. CLOAK sets out to watch the switching-off as it happens, cell by cell, in tumour models grown from patients' own tissue.

01 · Grow

Tumours in a dish

Small, living copies of each patient's tumour, called organoids, grown from biopsy tissue with consent.

02 · Film

Watch them meet

The patient's own T cells are added and filmed for days under the microscope, to see exactly when and how they go quiet.

03 · Retrain

Switch them back on

Combinations of existing drugs are tested to find which ones keep the T cells working, patient by patient.

For patients

Thinking about taking part?

If you're having a biopsy at one of our hospitals, your care team may ask whether a small extra piece of tissue can be used for research. Saying no never affects your treatment.

What taking part involves
  • No extra proceduresTissue comes from a biopsy you're already having.
  • You can change your mindWithdraw at any time, without giving a reason.
  • You'll hear what we findA plain-language update every year, if you want one.
